🧬 Renal Genetics · Dx & Tx Podocytopathy + Hereditary

Diagnostic and therapeutic guide for hereditary glomerular diseases — FSGS, Alport spectrum, ADPKD, APOL1 kidney disease, CoQ10‑responsive podocytopathies, and pedigree analysis.

📋 Diagnostic algorithm

Stepwise approach to genetic testing in glomerular and cystic kidney disease.

  1. Confirm phenotype — nephrotic syndrome, FSGS, hematuria, cystic disease, or unexplained CKD.
  2. Assess age of onset & family history — early onset (<50 yr) significantly raises pretest probability [citation:8].
  3. Exclude secondary causes — autoimmune, monoclonal, infection, drugs (see table).
  4. Check steroid responsiveness — SSNS → no genetic testing. SRNS → proceed.
  5. Look for extrarenal clues — hearing loss, ocular abnormalities, neurologic features, Wilms tumor, liver cysts.
  6. Order targeted NGS panel — first-line. WES/WGS if panel negative or atypical.
  7. Interpret variants — use ClinGen curation (Definitive/Strong vs VUS).
  8. Act on diagnosis — CoQ10, RAS blockade, immunosuppression avoidance, surveillance, family screening.
🔬 When to test — guideline consensus:
✔️ Congenital, infantile, or syndromic nephrotic syndrome.
✔️ Primary SRNS (children & adults).
✔️ FSGS with family history, syndromic features, or steroid resistance.
✔️ Persistent hematuria, proteinuria, unexplained kidney failure, or FSGS of unknown origin (Alport workup) [citation:1][citation:13].
✔️ Cystic kidney disease (ADPKD).
❌ Not in steroid‑sensitive nephrotic syndrome (including familial).

🌳 Pedigree analysis

A well‑constructed pedigree is the highest‑yield “genetic test” and guides inheritance pattern, testing strategy, and donor selection.

📐 Key symbols & patterns
  • □ ○ Unaffected male / female
  • ■ ● Affected male / female
  • ◊ Sex unknown
  • — Consanguinity (double line)

🔍 Inheritance patterns to recognize

X‑linkedMales affected > females; no male‑to‑male transmission; carrier females may have hematuriaCOL4A5 (Alport)

⚠️ Pitfalls: Incomplete penetrance (e.g., APOL1 — only ~15–20% of high‑risk carriers develop overt nephropathy [citation:9]), phenotypic variability, and de novo mutations can mask inheritance. Do not exclude sporadic cases from testing based solely on negative family history [citation:2].

👪 Family screening implications

  • Genetic diagnosis enables presymptomatic screening of at‑risk relatives [citation:2].
  • Living‑donor evaluation: COL4A3/A4 heterozygous carriers should be considered for donation as last possible resource [citation:1][citation:13].
  • Family planning — prenatal and preimplantation genetic diagnosis options.

📌 When to consider genetic analysis

Based on recent CKD genetics guidelines and expert consensus [citation:8].

✅ Strongly recommended

  • CKD diagnosed before age 50 without clear nongenetic cause (yield up to ~20%) [citation:8]
  • Family history of kidney disease or consanguineous parents
  • Extrarenal features suggesting a syndromic disorder (hearing loss, ocular, neurologic, skeletal)
  • Kidney phenotype suggestive of a known genetic disorder — cystic kidney disease, Alport spectrum, tubulopathies, congenital nephropathies
  • Proteinuric glomerulopathies where genetic diagnosis guides therapy (e.g., SRNS, FSGS, aHUS)
  • Unusual disease course or unexplained CKD
  • Persistent hematuria, proteinuria, or unexplained kidney failure (COL4A3/4/5 testing) [citation:1][citation:13]
  • FSGS of unknown origin (COL4A testing) [citation:1]

👪 Family members & donors

  • Relatives of patients with a known genetic cause
  • Potential living kidney donors with family history or hematuria
  • Patients of reproductive age with concerns about transmission [citation:8]
💡 Key reversal: Genetic testing is now recommended (2021 KDIGO / 2023 KDOQI) for adults with FSGS who have familial kidney disease, syndromic features, or steroid resistance — a reversal from the 2012 stance. Treatment resistance is a strong indicator of genetic etiology [citation:2].

🩸 APOL1 kidney disease

Risk modifier — incomplete penetrance

🧬 Genetics

G1 and G2 risk variants in APOL1 (chromosome 22) — common in individuals of African ancestry. Toxic gain‑of‑function disrupts ion channels, mitochondrial function, and inflammasome activation [citation:9].

📊 Penetrance & triggers

  • Incomplete penetrance — only ~15–20% of high‑risk genotype carriers develop overt nephropathy [citation:9]
  • Requires secondary triggers: HIV infection (HIVAN), interferon exposure, SARS‑CoV‑2, EBV, parvovirus B19, malaria [citation:9]
  • Associated with FSGS, HIVAN, hypertension‑attributed kidney failure; association with diabetic kidney disease is inconsistent [citation:9]

🔬 Testing guidance (KDIGO 2025) [citation:15]

  • Insufficient evidence for population‑level screening
  • Genotyping can inform risk stratification, follow‑up frequency, living donation, and clinical trial eligibility
  • Genotype results are not by themselves actionable — no established targeted therapy yet

💊 Management

  • Aggressive BP control and RAS blockade
  • Avoid interferon therapy; aggressive HIV treatment
  • Emerging: inaxaplin (VX‑147) — ~47% UPCR reduction in Phase 2a (open‑label, small); Phase 3 AMPLITUDE ongoing [citation:9]
  • MZE829 — ~35.6% albuminuria reduction in Phase 2 HORIZON (open‑label, small) [citation:9]

⚠️ No APOL1‑targeted agent has yet demonstrated benefit on hard kidney endpoints [citation:9].

🫘 ADPKD — Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease

Most common monogenic kidney disorder (~1:1000) [citation:10].

🧬 Genetics

GeneFrequencyProteinNotes
PKD1~85%Polycystin‑1More severe, earlier ESRD
PKD2~15%Polycystin‑2Milder, later onset
PKD3RareUnmappedVery rare

Two‑hit mechanism: germline mutation (first hit) + somatic second hit → focal cyst formation [citation:4].

🔍 Diagnosis & testing

  • Imaging — ultrasound, MRI (cyst count/volume)
  • Genetic testing — for atypical cases, early onset, negative imaging with strong family history, or reproductive planning
  • Pedigree — AD pattern; vertical transmission

💊 Management

  • BP control (ACEi/ARB preferred)
  • High water intake
  • Tolvaptan — slows cyst growth in selected patients (monitor LFTs)
  • Pain management; cyst hemorrhage/infection treatment
  • Transplant evaluation when ESRD approaches

🩺 Alport spectrum & FSGS — focused clinical guidance

Alport syndrome (COL4A3/4/5)

🚩 When to suspect: Persistent hematuria + proteinuria, sensorineural hearing loss, ocular abnormalities (lenticonus, retinopathy), FSGS of unknown origin, or unexplained CKD. Test COL4A3/4/5 as part of initial workup [citation:1][citation:13].
InheritanceGene(s)Severity
X‑linkedCOL4A5Highest risk; males severe
Autosomal recessiveCOL4A3/A4 (biallelic)Severe
Autosomal dominantCOL4A3/A4 (heterozygous)Variable; may be mild → FSGS

Treatment: Early RAS blockade is standard [citation:1][citation:13]. SGLT2 inhibitors may be added for adults with proteinuria and CKD [citation:1][citation:13]. Do not use immunosuppression — these are structural defects.

FSGS — when genetic vs. immune?

  • Genetic FSGS: SRNS, family history, syndromic features, no response to steroids/CNIs. Avoid immunosuppression.
  • Immune‑mediated FSGS (e.g., tip variant): May respond to steroids, rituximab, CNIs. Biopsy is essential — genetic testing and biopsy can give discordant results (e.g., COL4A4 variant + steroid‑responsive tip variant FSGS) [citation:7].
  • Genetic testing in FSGS of unknown origin: COL4A3/4/5 should be included [citation:1].
⚠️ Biopsy still matters: A patient can have both a genetic variant (e.g., Alport) and an immune‑mediated FSGS that responds to immunosuppression. Treatment decisions require integrating genetics, biopsy, and clinical course [citation:7].
